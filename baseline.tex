In this section, we first outline the sequential algorithm of a 
single time step of LESGO. Then, we describe its MPI parallelization and 
analyze the scaling limits that motivate our GPU acceleration strategy.

\subsection{Structure of a Time Step}
\label{sec:step}

LESGO advances the three filtered velocity components of
Eq.~\ref{eq:les} through a standard time marching loop
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:step}). At each step, the solver evaluates the 
right-hand-side (RHS) terms of Eq.~\ref{eq:les}, updates the 
velocity explicitly, and then solves a pressure equation to keep 
the flow incompressible. Algorithm~\ref{alg:step} shows the main
computational components of LESGO, including derivatives, SGS,
convection, turbines, and pressure, which together account for most of
the step time (Fig.~\ref{fig:stagescale}). For brevity, the ellipses stand for the remaining routines, such as
adaptive time step control and explicit time integration, and 
our GPU port applies the same porting approach to them. Below, we
first describe the computation in each of these components.
Sections~\ref{sec:method} and~\ref{sec:opt} present how we
optimize them on GPUs.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{The main time loop of LESGO, with other routines elided.}
\label{alg:step}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \texttt{Initialize()}
\FOR{$n = 1, N$}
\STATE $\ldots$
\STATE \textcolor{stgderiv}{Derivatives()}\algptr{stgderiv}{sec:method-taxonomy}
\STATE \textcolor{stgsgs}{SGS()}
\STATE \hspace{1.3em}\textbf{if} $n \bmod c = 0$ \textbf{then}
\STATE \hspace{2.6em}\textcolor{stgsgs}{UpdateLASDCoefficient()}\algptr{stgsgs}{sec:opt-batch}
\STATE \hspace{1.3em}\textcolor{stgsgs}{SGSStress()}
\STATE \hspace{1.3em}\textcolor{stgsgs}{StressDivergence()}
\STATE \textcolor{stgconv}{Convection()}\algptr{stgconv}{sec:opt-overlap}
\STATE \textcolor{stgturb}{Turbines()}
\STATE \hspace{1.3em}\textcolor{stgturb}{SampleBladeVelocities()}\algptr{stgturb}{sec:opt-batch}
\STATE \hspace{1.3em}\textcolor{stgturb}{ComputeBladeForces()}\algptr{stgturb}{sec:opt-overlap}
\STATE \hspace{1.3em}\textcolor{stgturb}{SpreadBladeForces()}\algptr{stgturb}{sec:opt-batch}
\STATE $\ldots$
\STATE \textcolor{stgpress}{Pressure()}
\STATE \hspace{1.3em}\textcolor{stgpress}{PoissonFFT()}
\STATE \hspace{1.3em}\textcolor{stgpress}{PoissonTridiag()}\algptr{stgpress}{sec:opt-tridag}
\STATE \hspace{1.3em}\textcolor{stgpress}{PoissonInvFFT()}
\STATE ProjectVelocity()
\ENDFOR
\STATE \texttt{Finalize()}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\stagelead{stgderiv}{Derivatives} (line 4). The convective and SGS terms
of Eq.~\ref{eq:les} need the velocity gradients, which form the
vorticity and the strain rate, respectively. On each horizontal $x$-$y$ plane, LESGO computes the
horizontal derivatives spectrally with a forward 2D FFT, a
multiplication by the wavenumbers, and an inverse 2D FFT. Vertical
derivatives use second-order centered differences between adjacent
planes. The stage therefore combines many 2D FFTs with streaming sweeps
over 3D arrays.

\stagelead{stgsgs}{SGS} (lines 5--9). The SGS model represents the effect
of turbulent motions smaller than the grid spacing. Every $c$-th step
($c$=5 in production runs), \texttt{UpdateLASDCoefficient()} recomputes
the LASD model coefficient (Section~\ref{sec:bg-physics}). It filters
21 3D fields at two filter widths with 2D FFTs and interpolates four fields along
fluid-particle trajectories, which makes it the most irregular and
memory-bound routine in the solver (Section~\ref{sec:opt-batch}). On
every step, \texttt{SGSStress()} computes the SGS stress from this
coefficient and the velocity gradients, and \texttt{StressDivergence()}
takes its divergence.

\stagelead{stgconv}{Convection} (line 10). To eliminate aliasing errors 
in the nonlinear convective term, LESGO employs 
the standard $3/2$-rule dealiasing scheme. It transforms each velocity
and vorticity component onto an expanded ($1.5\times$ padded) grid with
2D horizontal FFTs, forms the nonlinear product there, and transforms
the product back to the original grid. Convection represents the most 
FFT-intensive phase of the time step.

\stagelead{stgturb}{Turbines} (lines 11--14). The actuator-line model
(Section~\ref{sec:bg-physics}) couples the turbines to the flow in three
steps. \texttt{SampleBladeVelocities()} interpolates the flow velocity at
each blade point, \texttt{ComputeBladeForces()} looks up lift and drag
in tabulated airfoil data, and \texttt{SpreadBladeForces()} spreads the
resulting forces onto nearby grid cells with a Gaussian kernel. The
stage thus gathers from and scatters to the 3D grid at thousands of
off-grid blade points.

\stagelead{stgpress}{Pressure} (lines 16--19). The explicit time
integration leaves an intermediate velocity that is not divergence-free,
and LESGO restores incompressibility by solving a Poisson equation for
the pressure. \texttt{PoissonFFT()} applies 2D FFTs to every horizontal
plane, which splits the 3D equation into independent tridiagonal systems
along $z$, one per horizontal wavenumber pair. \texttt{PoissonTridiag()}
solves these systems with the Thomas algorithm
(Section~\ref{sec:opt-tridag}), and \texttt{PoissonInvFFT()} transforms
the pressure back to physical space. Finally,
\texttt{ProjectVelocity()} (line 20) subtracts the pressure gradient
from the velocity to make the flow divergence-free.

\subsection{MPI Slab Decomposition and Scaling Ceiling}
\label{sec:bg-impl}

LESGO is implemented in Fortran~90 and parallelized with MPI using a 1D slab 
decomposition along the vertical ($z$) axis. Each MPI process owns a contiguous 
block of horizontal $z$-planes spanning the full horizontal domain ($x$-$y$)
(Fig.~\ref{fig:lesgo-decomposition}a). 
This topology allows 2D horizontal FFTs to execute entirely within each process,
completely avoiding expensive 2D domain transposes (\texttt{MPI\_Alltoallv})
required by 2D pencil decompositions~\cite{romero2022}. Primary physical 
fields, including velocity components, modified pressure, and subgrid 
turbulence scalars, are stored as 3D Fortran arrays.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \vspace{-0.1in}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/lesgo_domain_communication.pdf}
    \caption{LESGO's decomposition. (a) The $N_x \times N_y \times N_z$ grid is split along $z$ into $n$ slabs of $N_z/n$ contiguous $x$-$y$ planes. (b) Vertical finite-difference stencils copy each slab's boundary plane into its neighbor's ghost plane.}
    \label{fig:lesgo-decomposition}
\end{figure}


Inter-process communication in this slab topology follows three main patterns.
First, vertical finite-difference stencils require nearest-neighbor halo
exchanges, in which each process swaps a single horizontal plane with the
processes directly below and above it (Fig.~\ref{fig:lesgo-decomposition}b). \texttt{ProjectVelocity()} ends with such an exchange
to refresh the ghost planes for the next step. Second, the pressure
Poisson solve chains all processes together. Each vertical tridiagonal
system spans the entire domain height, so the distributed Thomas algorithm
passes boundary coefficients from the bottom process up to the top one
and back down (Section~\ref{sec:opt-tridag}).
Pressure is thus the main stage with fine-grained, globally coupled
communication. Third, small collective reductions (\texttt{MPI\_Allreduce})
run over all processes for the adaptive Courant--Friedrichs--Lewy (CFL)
time step control and runtime diagnostic logging, and over only the
processes each turbine spans for per-turbine force aggregation. By avoiding 2D
domain transposes, this 1D slab decomposition keeps
horizontal FFTs local to each process at the expense of a globally coupled 
vertical solve.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig_ceiling.pdf}
    \caption{CPU strong scaling of LESGO on the production case (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). 
    The cross marks 128 processes on a single node, which exceeds node memory.}
    \label{fig:ceiling}
    \vspace{-0.1in}
\end{figure}
    

On CPU architectures, this design encounters severe scaling limits.
Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling} evaluates the strong scaling of the production
wind-farm case, which simulates 60 turbines on a 604M-cell grid
($3072 \times 384 \times 512$), on Perlmutter CPU nodes with
two 64-core AMD EPYC 7763 (Milan) CPUs each.
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling}, increasing cores from
16 to 64 results in only 1.08$\times$ speedup, while 128 processes exhaust the node's memory. 
Four nodes at 512 processes plateau at 6.48\,s per step, only $2.1\times$ faster than a single node. 
At this scaling ceiling, accumulating converged turbulence statistics
require hundreds of thousands of time steps. As a result, a single
production run takes several days or even months to complete, making
large-scale wind-farm studies computationally impractical. This significant
performance gap motivated our development of the GPU port.
