This section first validates the GPU port against the CPU version and
then evaluates solver performance on the production wind-farm
benchmark. We present cumulative speedups and analyze strong and weak
scaling behavior.

\subsection{Validation of the GPU Port}
\label{sec:results-validation}


Before we evaluate performance, we verify that the GPU port reproduces
the results of the CPU version. We use a small case with one NREL 5-MW
turbine, modeled by a panel-based actuator-line method with 120
actuator points per blade, and we disable both the SGS model and
molecular viscosity. The CPU and GPU runs share the same cold start,
$480\times384\times240$ grid, fixed nondimensional time step
$\Delta t=0.0014$, and 1,000 steps. The CPU run uses 48 MPI processes,
and the GPU run uses two A100 GPUs.

Figure~\ref{fig:lesgo-cpu-gpu-field} compares the instantaneous
near-wake fields of the two runs at the common physical time
$t=15.4737$\,s. The two fields agree to floating-point roundoff. The
wake-deficit pattern correlation is 1.0, the relative $L_2$ difference
is $1.47\times10^{-13}$, and the maximum normalized axial velocity
difference is $9.00\times10^{-14}$.  
The GPU-minus-CPU difference (Fig.~\ref{fig:lesgo-cpu-gpu-field}c) remains
at the roundoff level. The hub-height centerline profiles
(Fig.~\ref{fig:lesgo-cpu-gpu}) are also indistinguishable.  
With the LASD model enabled, the two versions agree to
roundoff over the first several steps, and their time-averaged
statistics agree within sampling uncertainty. These results confirm
that the GPU port preserves the numerical behavior of the CPU version.

Table~\ref{tab:cumulative} shows how the performance of LESGO improves as we apply the optimizations one by one. Each row
builds on the previous one, so the speedups accumulate. 
% We label each row with the section that describes its optimization. 
First, fine-grained
explicit residency (\S\ref{sec:method-placement}) brings the step time
down to 1.32\,s, already $4.9\times$ faster than the fastest CPU
configuration.
Then, relocating the actuator-line model onto the device
(\S\ref{sec:method-taxonomy}) removes the last recurring full-field
data movement over PCIe and further reduces the step time to
0.574\,s. Chunking and pipelining the
distributed tridiagonal solve (\S\ref{sec:opt-tridag}) converts the
serialized process chain of the pressure solver into overlapped chunk
waves for an additional $1.9\times$. Batching small work items into a few
kernels and packed reductions (\S\ref{sec:opt-batch}) cuts the
launch and collective overhead for another $1.6\times$. Finally,
heterogeneous CPU--GPU execution
(\S\ref{sec:opt-overlap}) contributes a $1.2\times$ speedup. Each
optimization removes a distinct bottleneck.
The fully optimized GPU solver achieves 0.162\,s per time step (0.27\,ms
per million cells), representing a $40\times$ speedup over the fastest
CPU configuration. 
The bottom two rows of Table~\ref{tab:cumulative} separately report
the performance with the LASD coefficient update enabled at $q=5$
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Our GPU version again delivers a
$39.7\times$ speedup over the CPU version.

\subsection{Strong and Weak Scaling}
\label{sec:results-scaling}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig_scaling.pdf}
\caption{Strong scaling of the wind-farm case at three problem
sizes. Labels on the 604M curve give the speedup over the fastest CPU
configuration (6.48\,s per step, Fig.~\ref{fig:ceiling}).}
\label{fig:scaling}
\vspace{-0.05in}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} presents strong scaling for the 60-turbine wind
farm across vertical grid resolutions of 512, 768, and 1152 levels. All
three problem sizes show robust strong scaling, and the 1.36B-cell
grid scales out to 128 GPUs. On the production 604M-cell grid, 64 GPUs 
run it $2.2\times$ faster than 16 GPUs, at 0.072\,s per step. At this 
scale, the GPU solver runs 90$\times$ faster than the fastest CPU 
configuration (Table~\ref{tab:cumulative}).

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig_stagescale.pdf}
    \caption{Breakdown of GPU LESGO step time by stage on the 604M-cell
    grid. \texttt{Others} is the rest of the measured step time.}
    % \texttt{Other} includes the velocity projection and the routines elided
    % in Algorithm~\ref{alg:step}.}
    \label{fig:stagescale}
    \vspace{-0.05in}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:stagescale} breaks down the step time on the 604M-cell
grid by stage. We measure each stage by its kernel time, and
\texttt{Others} is the rest of the measured step time, including the
kernels of routines elided in Algorithm~\ref{alg:step}. 
% On 16 GPUs, convection is the largest stage (43\% of kernel time). SGS
% (23\%), derivatives (16\%), and the pressure solver (11\%) follow.
From 16 to 64 GPUs, the kernel time of every stage drops by
3.2--4.0$\times$, close to the ideal. From 16 to 32 GPUs,
\texttt{Others} stays at about 18\,ms, which reflects a fixed per-step
cost of kernel launches and the collectives of the adaptive time step, 
whose cost depends on the turbine count and the blade
discretization rather than on the grid size.
On 64 GPUs, however, \texttt{Others} grows to 31\,ms. We attribute
this to the small workload per process, which is no longer large
enough to hide the host cost.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig_roofline.pdf}
\caption{Roofline of the LESGO kernels in one production step on an
A100 GPU (Perlmutter). Colors give the stage in Algorithm~\ref{alg:step},
and open circles mark cuFFT kernels, which serve several stages.}
\label{fig:roofline}
\vspace{-0.05in}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:roofline} demonstrates the kernels of one production step on
the roofline model~\cite{williams2009roofline}. Most kernels line up along the memory roof, and kernels
that sustain more than 70\% of peak DRAM bandwidth account for 87\% of
kernel time. 
cuFFT kernels take 62\% of kernel time and run at 78\% of peak
bandwidth. Aggregated by stage,
every stage except the turbines sustains 77--81\% of peak bandwidth. The only
kernels below the roof are the actuator-line kernels. We attribute this to their irregular access to grid cells around off-grid blade points.
These kernels take only 0.7\% of kernel time, so their overall effect is
negligible.
% They perform many floating-point operations per byte of DRAM traffic, which places them right of the ridge point.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig_scaling_weak.pdf}
\caption{Weak scaling at three per-GPU loads on Perlmutter.}
\label{fig:weakscaling}
\vspace{-0.1in}

Figure~\ref{fig:weakscaling} reports weak scaling, where each GPU retains
a fixed grid size and the domain grows vertically with process count.
At the production load of 29.5M cells per GPU the port sustains 90\%
parallel efficiency at 64 GPUs (149.4 to 165.7\,ms per step from 4 to
64 GPUs). The other two problem sizes exhibit similarly high weak-scaling efficiency. 
% This confirms that scaling efficiency is maintained as long as
% each GPU carries a sufficiently large workload, which is the regime
% that production runs occupy. 
These results demonstrate the effectiveness
of the optimizations presented in Sections~\ref{sec:method} and~\ref{sec:opt}.
